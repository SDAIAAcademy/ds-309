Personal data in an analysis

01
Recap from DS-207

The law asks for a purpose first, then only the data that purpose needs

Purpose

Data collected to decide support is used for that, or for a compatible purpose under the rules.

Minimisation

Each field that leaves has a reason tied to the purpose. "Might be useful" is not one.

Secondary use

Research use has its own rules: legitimate purpose, minimum necessary, an approved request.

This course goes further. DS-207 decided whether a request is lawful. Here you measure how identifying the data still is after you cut it, and you reduce that risk with numbers to show for it.

Two minutes only. If the room has not taken DS-207, name the three ideas and move on: the Personal Data Protection Law and its Implementing Regulation are the reference, and the General Rules for Secondary Use of Data cover research use. Sources: PDPL and Implementing Regulation (SDAIA); General Rules for Secondary Use of Data (SDAIA).

Not every field identifies the same way

Direct identifiers, quasi-identifiers and sensitive attributes

KindWhat it doesIn the Yusr table
Direct identifierPoints at one person by itselfnational identifier, name, mobile, e-mail, the fund's applicant number
Quasi-identifierPoints at one person in combination, and someone outside could know itbirth date, gender, postcode, district, nationality, household size
Sensitive attributeWhat an attacker wants to learnincome, social security, disability in the household, the fund's decision
Free textCan hold any of the abovethe officers' case notes

Ask: is the applicant number a direct identifier? Most say no because it is not a name. It is: inside the fund it opens the whole file, and outside it is a key to it. The four tags are the ones of Lab 1.1. Sensitive here is used in the disclosure-control sense (what harms the person if learnt); the PDPL's own list of sensitive data is narrower, so do not equate the two.

How to tell a quasi-identifier

Ask who outside the fund could already know the value

  • A neighbour knows the postcode and roughly the household size
  • An employer knows the sector and the birth date
  • A public list may hold name, birth date, gender and postcode together
  • If an outsider can know it, an outsider can join on it

Three ordinary fields

In 1990 United States census data, 87% of people were likely unique on five-digit ZIP code, gender and date of birth.

The Sweeney figure is the classic one: 216 million of 248 million people. Do not over-read it for Saudi postcodes; Lab 1.4 measures the same thing on the Yusr table, and the result there is even starker. Sources: Sweeney, Simple Demographics Often Identify People Uniquely (2000).

A code is not anonymity

A pseudonymised extract is still personal data

  • Pseudonymisation replaces identifiers with codes; the key is kept apart
  • Whoever holds the key can reverse it; the quasi-identifiers stay
  • SDAIA's anonymisation guideline treats pseudonymised data as personal data and its protection as weaker than anonymisation
  • Anonymised data must be irreversible, with the re-identification risk assessed

A hash is a code too

Anyone with a list of national identifiers recomputes the same hashes and finds the rows (Lab 1.1 stretch).

The guideline names generalisation, aggregation, encryption and masking as example techniques, and asks for an assessment of re-identification potential when data is anonymised. Days 1 and 2 are that assessment, done with numbers. Sources: Personal Data Destruction, Anonymization and Pseudonymisation Guideline, version 1.0 (SDAIA, 2024).

When E2 may leave

A research extract leaves under conditions, not because names are gone

1 · Requestthrough the researcher's university, with its written approval
→
2 · Purposeresearch or public interest, not profit
→

Steps 1 to 3 restate the General Rules for Secondary Use of Data in plain words: a legitimate purpose that serves the public interest or research and excludes profit, data confined to the minimum necessary, and for a researcher a request through the institution with written evidence of approval. Step 4 is the fund's own control and the subject of Day 2. Sources: General Rules for Secondary Use of Data (SDAIA).

The controls a release can use

Five controls, each with a different guarantee and a different cost

ControlWhat it guaranteesWhat it costs
Pseudonymisationnothing outside the key holder; still personal dataalmost nothing
De-identificationeach record hides among at least k on the chosen fieldsdetail, and rows suppressed
Aggregationno row-level datano row-level analysis; small cells still leak
Synthetic datano real row, if the generator does not copyweaker relationships; must be tested
Differential privacya bound on what any one person changesnoise, and a budget that runs out

This table is the backbone of LO1 and of the decision table participants write in task 4 of Lab 1.2. Ask which control gives a guarantee that does not depend on what the attacker knows: only differential privacy. k-anonymity's guarantee holds only for the fields you chose as quasi-identifiers.

Fit the control to the release

The use decides the control, and the control decides the risk left

  • Follow one applicant over years inside the fund: pseudonymisation
  • Row-level records for an approved study: de-identification
  • Once-a-year regional counts: aggregation
  • A realistic file for a hackathon: synthetic data
  • Monthly district counts on a public dashboard: differential privacy

Residual risk is part of the answer

The monthly dashboard spends its budget twelve times a year. The hackathon file is only as safe as the generator's leakage test.

These are the five releases of Lab 1.2 task 4, so do not dwell: participants will write them. The right column is the part most people skip: every control leaves a risk, and the decision table must name it.

Who the attacker is

Assume the attacker knows someone who applied

Prosecutor

Knows a specific person is in the release and looks for their row.

Journalist

Has an outside file and links whoever links; does not know who applied.

Insider

A relative or colleague who knows exact values: birth date, household size, sector.

Lab 1.3 played the journalist. The prosecutor is stronger: they already know the person is in the release, so a unique record is a certain hit. Lab 1.4's average risk (one over the class size) is the prosecutor's chance. Plan for the insider when you choose quasi-identifiers: anything a relative knows is a quasi-identifier.

From Lab 1.4. Release A is worse than Sweeney's census finding: 99.1% unique, because a fund's applicants are a small population spread over many postcodes. The punchline is the last line: k alone says all three are equally bad. Read unique, below five and average risk together.

What k tells you

k is a floor for the most exposed person, not an average

  • k = 5: every record hides among at least five
  • One unique record makes k = 1 for the whole release
  • Report k with the share below the target and the average risk
  • The fund's target in this course: k of at least 5

Average risk

The mean of one over the class size: the chance that a prosecutor picks the right record. Release C: 0.004. Its maximum is still 1.

Why five? It is a common choice, not a law. Write the target and its reason in the release log; Day 4's datasheet asks for it. Some organisations use 10 for health data. The threshold matters less than measuring and reporting it honestly.

When k is met and the harm happens anyway

A class of five that all share one value discloses it to anyone who finds the class

Homogeneity

In release B, 661 of 3,241 classes of five or more have a single social security value (Lab 1.4 stretch).

l-diversity

Ask every class to hold at least l different values of the sensitive attribute. A partial fix: two values can still be "yes" and "almost yes".

This is the limit of k-anonymity that the topic list asks for. The stronger notion, t-closeness, asks each class's distribution of the sensitive value to be close to the whole table's; mention it by name only. Differential privacy (Day 2) is the answer that does not depend on which fields you thought of.

Tools for disclosure risk

Write it in pandas to see the mechanism, then use a tool for the release

sizes = df.groupby(qi)[qi[0]].transform("size")
unique   = (sizes == 1).sum()
k        = sizes.min()
below_5  = (sizes < 5).mean()
avg_risk = (1 / sizes).mean()

ARX

A desktop and Java tool: hierarchies, k, l, t, risk models and utility.

sdcMicro

An R package for statistical disclosure control with risk and utility measures.

Five lines are the whole of Lab 1.4's computation. The tools are interchangeable examples of the capability slot "de-identification and disclosure risk"; both were checked on their official pages this month (ARX site; sdcMicro 5.8.2 on CRAN). Sources: ARX data anonymization tool; sdcMicro on CRAN.
